nimisin, and Ones I Actually Like

written November 16, 2026

What is a nimisin?

If you're reading this article, it's really unlikely you need a full write up on what toki pona (the constructed language started by Sonja Lang in 2001) and nimisin are, but in case you do, here's a quick summary.

toki pona as a conlang is special due to it having a comically small "word inventory", on the order of ~130 total words. For context, Classical Latin, a very old language with a very low word count, has ~40,000. English today has ~880,000.

"Digi, if there's so few words in the language, how do you speak in it, and also why do you not know the number exactly?"

I'll answer the second question first: basically, there's a lot of contention as to which words "count." The original book "Toki Pona: The Language of Good" (also called pu) lists 123 words. The later "Toki Pona Dictionary" (also called ku) lists 137. The site nimi.li lists 119 "core" words, plus 10 "common" words, plus 12 "uncommon" words, for a total of 141. Then, there's 37 "obscure" words, all with at least over 5% usage. Then, there's the whopping 188 "sandbox" words; of course a lot of those are jokes, and none of them see much use.

As for how to speak the language? I don't have the time, frankly, to explain that one. TL;DR: you have to use the small amount of words to explain what you mean. Sure, there's no word for cake, but I can say "pan suwi musi" (lit. "fun sweet bread") and you'll get what I mean. If we were already talking about birthdays, you'll definitely get what I mean.

So what are nimisin? nimisin, from the words nimi (meaning "word") and sin (meaning "new"), refer to "new words" in the language, but the word has a... derogatory feel to it. On the whole, the majority of toki pona speakers don't love the idea of nimisin, or if they do, they use a very small amount of them. Why? Because toki pona has a philosophy of minimalism.

Now, frankly, I'm not going to get too much into the philosophy stuff. You can have an interesting discussion about minimalism, if toki pona is a good example of it, if it even should be minimalist, and if it does a good job at either minimalism or what it set out to do. I'm not really interested in that discussion; frankly, toki pona has grown well past its creator, and I think it's more intersting to discuss what it is vs. what it was/is "trying" to be. It's a language people use to speak, and its design choices affect the way it's spoken. There's definitely some really cool effects to the language having so few words, and I think that's what draws a lot of people in. I know for me, that was a big influence into wanting to learn it. I have never been especially good at language acquisition, but within just a couple months of learning toki pona, I was able to have (slow, unsure) conversations in text with fellow learners. It's a lot of fun, and I think "this language is fun and makes you think about the words you're using" is it's biggest strong suit.

But there's definitely a case to be made that adding too many words to this language would "defeat the purpose," right? If we're going to sit here and say that one of the advantages of speaking a language like this is to force you to explain yourself with very few available words, if we suddenly add a word for "cake", that whole "contextual breakdown of the concept of cake" isn't needed anymore. So obviously, we should be conversative with how many new words we come up with.

Some words that are common in the language now used to be nimisin. In fact, a good chunk of the current inventory wasn't invented by Sonja Lang herself, but instead by the greater toki pona community. For example, tonsi, coined in 2019 by jan inwin, a word meaning nonbinary, is nowadays considered a common enough word that 77% of speakers recognize and use it. So clearly, there are some concepts that can't be covered (or at least covered well) with the ~120 original words.

So, now we get to the point of this page: what obscure or sandbox words might be good additions to the language as a whole? Well, I'm no expert, which I'll get into in a second, but these are just the ones I like. I definitely don't even use all of these, and some of them are just fun, but we're here to have some fun, so I don't see the harm.

At the end of the day, even adding every obscure word to the language and every sandbox word with even any serious backing behind it keeps the inventory well below 200, which is just fine in my book.

But again, I'm not an expert; I've been speaking for about three months, and observing for only a few years, so my opinions are just that: opinions. I asked a couple of my friends, as well-- Tate (ijo Teto) and Arceus (soweli Asijesi) for their opinions too. I'll note when their takes come up.

I fully expect my opinions to change on this as time goes on! I might even document those feelings as I learn the language more here on this page.

The nimisin

puwa

puwa

obscure (12%)

jan Seli, 2021

 puwa is a word that means fluffy, soft, or squishy. Like a pillow, or slime, or a cloud. In traditional toki pona, this is encompassed by ko; but ko also includes things like powder, sand, dirt... and I don't know, I just feel like sand and pillows are squishy in such different ways that saying that they are both "kinds of ko" feels weird.
I expect some people to contest this, given that people are often wary of words that "bite into" the semantic space of other words (in this case ko, but I feel like puwa stands on it's own as a very different sensation.

jami

jami

obscure (9%)

jan inwin, 2017

 jami means yummy, but also just any positive sensory experience, including stimming. I dig this one for a few reasons -- it's fun, obviously, and I feel like one of the words with way too wide of a semantic space is the titular pona, which means good, but also "organized, simple, positive, happy, nice, friendly, peaceful, civil..."
I frankly understand pona's wideness, but jami takes so little out of pona's semantic space that I feel like it's a useful word in the language, and it matches other toki pona words in "you think means one thing, but has a lot of other uses." I can listen to my favorite song and say it's jami. I can jump into a warm bed and it's a jami experience. It's a good word!
It's also jan jami's name, and they're cool. jan [jami:] is a great sitelen ponization.
